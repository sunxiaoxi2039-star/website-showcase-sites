Field notes

Why a flower counts

A sunflower head is built from the centre outward. Each new floret—later a seed—appears at the growing edge, turned from its predecessor by the same divergence angle. Nothing else is specified. The spirals, the counts, the tight packing: all of it follows from that one number.

I · The rule

One angle, one square root

Helmut Vogel’s 1979 model places seed n at angle n × α and radius c times the square root of n. The square root keeps the area per seed constant, so the head fills evenly from centre to rim.

Everything you see on the plate above is those two expressions evaluated 987 times. The seeds are ellipses turned to face outward, sized a little larger toward the rim, as in the real flower.

II · The angle

The most irrational number

Suppose α is a simple fraction of a turn—3/8, 2/5, 5/13—seeds eventually retrace the same headings and pile into straight rays, leaving dark wedges of wasted space.

The golden angle, 360° × (2 − φ), about 137.508°, has a continued fraction of nothing but ones. No fraction approximates it well, so no heading ever repeats and every new gap is filled.

III · The count

Spirals in Fibonacci pairs

Your eye links each seed to its nearest neighbours and reads spirals—parastichies—running both ways. At the golden angle their counts are consecutive Fibonacci numbers: 21 and 34 near the heart, 55 and 89 at the rim.

Hover any seed on the plate: its nearest neighbours are exactly 55 and 89 seeds away in the sequence. Turn on Trace arms to colour one whole family of spirals.
