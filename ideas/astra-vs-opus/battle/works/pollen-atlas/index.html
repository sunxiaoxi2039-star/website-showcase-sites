Art study. Interpretive, not diagnostic.

A sunflower pollen grain is roughly thirty micrometres across — about a third the width of a human hair. Enlarged, it stops reading as dust and starts reading as building: a thick outer wall, the exine, studded with conical spines and cut by three furrows through which a pollen tube can emerge.

The spines are not ornament. They catch in the hairs of bees, and the oily pollenkitt coating the grain is what turns a forager’s legs gold. The palette here borrows that field colour rather than the slide’s — under a transmitted-light microscope the grain is pale and nearly transparent.

Read this plate as a sculpture, not a record. Spine count, lighting and colour are chosen for the eye; proportions follow published descriptions of the species, not any single specimen. The scale bar is honest to the model, not to a photograph.

Turn it. Palynologists nudge the coverslip to roll a grain and see it from the pole and from the equator; the controls above do the same.
